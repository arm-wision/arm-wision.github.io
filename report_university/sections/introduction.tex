\chapter{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Vegetation surveys underpin plant biodiversity monitoring: expert botanists place standardised quadrats (typically $50\times50$\,cm sampling frames) at field sites and identify every species visible within the frame. The procedure is accurate but slow, and it scales only with the limited supply of trained taxonomists~\cite{lifeclef2026}. The PlantCLEF 2026 challenge~\cite{plantclef2026, lifeclef2026} asks whether this task can be automated. Given a high resolution photograph of a quadrat, a system must predict the set of plant species present, drawn from a taxonomy of approximately 7,800 candidates. Submissions are scored by a macro averaged F1 computed per quadrat and averaged first within and then across transects,
\[
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{T_i}
  \sum_{j=1}^{T_i}\mathrm{F1}^{j}\right),
\]
where $N$ is the number of transects, $T_i$ the number of quadrats in transect $i$, and $\mathrm{F1}^{j}$ the per image F1 over the predicted species set. Averaging within transects first prevents densely sampled sites from dominating the score.

Two properties of the benchmark make it difficult. The first is the domain shift between training and test data. The training set contains over one million single plant images from the PlantCLEF 2024 corpus, each showing an individual specimen with a single species label, whereas the test images are dense vegetation scenes in which many species overlap, occlude one another, and appear at varying scales and growth stages (Figure~\ref{fig:quadrats}). Models trained only on isolated specimens never encounter this visual complexity and transfer poorly to it. The second is the long tail of the label distribution: common species have thousands of training images while many rare species, often the most ecologically important ones, have fewer than ten. Standard cross entropy training consequently favours common classes, yielding high micro accuracy but poor Macro F1.

This report describes the system submitted by the Australian National University team, which competed as \textbf{ARM Wision} on the official leaderboard. The design rests on three decisions. The first is partial fine-tuning of BioCLIP~2.5 with taxonomic regularisation. BioCLIP~\cite{stevens2024bioclip} is a contrastive vision language model whose pretraining on TreeOfLife-10M aligns its feature space with the Linnaean taxonomy. Because full fine-tuning collapses this prior, we unfreeze only the last four transformer blocks of the ViT-H/14 backbone and attach auxiliary cross entropy heads at the genus and family levels, with small fixed weights so that the auxiliary losses regularise the encoder rather than drive it.

The second decision is to enlarge the training manifest rather than rebalance it. We extend the training set to 2.65 million images with genus and family labels for every row. Capping each species at 500 images to flatten the head of the distribution lowered the public Macro F1 from 0.41826 to 0.40041, evidence that additional head class examples remain useful when label quality is high and that flattening discards information rather than rebalancing it.

The third decision is tile level inference with adaptive selection. Each quadrat is partitioned into a $4{\times}4$ grid of sixteen tiles, each tile is encoded at both $224$ and $336$ pixels, and the per tile softmax distributions are averaged across tiles and resolutions. Class prior logit adjustment ($\tau{=}0.25$) counteracts the long tail bias, and an adaptive threshold ($T{=}0.03$, with a floor of two and a ceiling of ten species) selects the final set. The dual resolution ensemble adds $+0.0066$ public Macro F1 over a single resolution, and adaptive selection outperforms the best fixed top-$k$ by about $0.007$.
