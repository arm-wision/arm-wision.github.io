\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Vegetation surveys underpin plant biodiversity monitoring: expert botanists place standardised quadrats (typically $50\times50$\,cm sampling frames) at field sites and identify every species visible within the frame. The procedure is accurate but slow, and it scales only with the limited supply of trained taxonomists~\cite{lifeclef2026}. The PlantCLEF 2026 challenge~\cite{plantclef2026, lifeclef2026} asks whether it can be automated: given a high resolution photograph of a quadrat, a system must predict the set of plant species present, drawn from a taxonomy of roughly 7,800 candidates. Submissions are scored by a macro averaged F1 over the predicted species set, computed per quadrat and averaged first within and then across transects so that densely sampled sites do not dominate.

Two properties of the benchmark make it difficult. The first is the domain shift between training and test data. The training set contains over one million single plant images from the PlantCLEF 2024 corpus, each showing one specimen with a single label, whereas the test images are dense vegetation scenes in which many species overlap, occlude one another, and appear at varying scales and growth stages (Figure~\ref{fig:quadrats}). Models trained only on isolated specimens never encounter this complexity and transfer poorly to it. The second is the long tail of the label distribution: common species have thousands of training images while many rare species, often the most ecologically important ones, have fewer than ten, so standard cross entropy training favours common classes and yields high micro accuracy but poor macro F1.

This report describes the system submitted by the Australian National University team, which competed as \textbf{ARM Wision} on the official leaderboard. Its design rests on three decisions. First, we partially fine tune BioCLIP 2.5~\cite{stevens2024bioclip}, a contrastive vision language model whose pretraining on TreeOfLife-10M aligns its feature space with the Linnaean taxonomy: because full fine tuning collapses this prior, we unfreeze only the last four transformer blocks of the ViT-H/14 backbone and attach auxiliary genus and family heads whose small fixed weights regularise the encoder rather than drive it. Second, we enlarge the training manifest to 2.65 million labelled images rather than rebalance it; capping each species at 500 images to flatten the head of the distribution in fact lowered the public Macro F1 from 0.41826 to 0.40041, evidence that head class examples remain useful when label quality is high. Third, we use tile level inference with adaptive selection: each quadrat is split into a $4{\times}4$ grid of sixteen tiles, encoded at $224$ and $336$ pixels and averaged across tiles and resolutions, after which class prior logit adjustment ($\tau{=}0.25$) and an adaptive probability threshold ($T{=}0.03$, between two and ten species) select the emitted set. The dual resolution ensemble adds $+0.0066$ public Macro F1 over a single resolution, and adaptive selection beats the best fixed top-$k$ by about $0.007$.
